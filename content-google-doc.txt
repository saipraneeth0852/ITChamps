THE BUSINESS CONTEXT
 
 
Nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions are among the most closely regulated byproducts of thermal power generation. Regulatory frameworks - from national environmental agencies to international ESG reporting standards - require power operators to monitor, report, and progressively reduce NOx output. Non-compliance carries financial penalties. But beyond regulatory risk, there is a growing strategic dimension: institutional investors, offtake partners, and public stakeholders are paying closer attention to emissions performance than at any previous point in the industry's history.
The conventional response to this monitoring requirement has been hardware-based: Continuous Emissions Monitoring Systems (CEMS) installed directly on flue gas stacks. These systems are effective - but they are expensive to procure, complex to maintain, and sensitive to the operational variability that characterises real-world combustion environments. For operators managing multiple units or seeking to extend monitoring capability without proportional capital spend, hardware CEMS presents a meaningful constraint.
This pioneering thermal power operator chose to challenge that constraint. The question it put to ITchamps was direct: could an AI model, trained on plant operational data, deliver monitoring accuracy equivalent to hardware CEMS - with regulatory approval to match?
 
THE CHALLENGE
 
 
NOx emissions in a thermal power plant are not a fixed output. They are a function of combustion dynamics - shaped continuously by fuel quality, air-to-fuel ratios, burner configuration, load conditions, and dozens of additional operational variables. That variability is precisely what makes emissions management difficult and what has historically made accurate monitoring dependent on direct physical measurement.
 
Unstable Control Parameters

	Compliance at Reasonable Cost

The core technical challenge was one of substitution under scrutiny. A software-based monitoring approach would only be viable if it could demonstrate to regulators that its emissions estimates were accurate enough to serve as the basis for compliance reporting. EPA approval - the formal recognition that a soft telemetry approach meets regulatory standards - was not a given. It had to be earned through demonstrated analytical performance across the full range of plant operating conditions.

THE BUSINESS CONTEXT

These packages are soldered face-down, with all connection points hidden beneath the component body after reflow. The only non-destructive method to evaluate them is X-ray inspection — and the standard governing what passes is unforgiving: IPC-7095D sets void area limits at less than 25% per ball for commercial production, and under 10% for defence and aerospace applications.
Vinyas Innovative Technologies Pvt. Ltd., based in Mysuru, manufactures PCBs for a range of industry applications — including products where joint reliability is non-negotiable. That era has passed.
As production volumes and BGA component density have grown, the gap between what manual inspection can deliver and what the business requires has widened. ITchamps was engaged to close it — not by replacing the Quada 3, but by building the AI intelligence layer that transforms it from a semi-automated viewer into a fully automated quality assurance system.
 
THE CHALLENGE
 
The root of the problem is structural, not procedural. The Quada 3's built-in semi-auto detection works only on high-contrast, clearly visible solder balls. Any ball falling in a 'shadow zone' — obscured by the component body or an underlying layer — is invisible to the machine's native software. A typical BGA package contains up to 2,000 solder balls. A single PCB may carry five to twelve BGA components.
